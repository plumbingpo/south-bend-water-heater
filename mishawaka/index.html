Keeping Your Mishawaka Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush every year in hard-water areas.

Equipment age plays a big role. With regular service, a standard tank typically lasts 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like discolored hot water, popping noises, or moisture around the base, it's time to plan a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule is cheaper and causes far less stress than scrambling for a new heater with water spreading across the floor.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water begins corroding the tank steel, and suddenly a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a flooded utility room.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Mishawaka

Most water heater problems we see in Mishawaka come back to the water itself. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Age matters just as much. Plenty of homes in the area still have the original tank, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod is usually spent and rust begins attacking the steel from the inside. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Code requirements add another layer. Replacing an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: an expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, including extra sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that occasionally develops when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper to address early.
